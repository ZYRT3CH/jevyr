# Jevyr launch post drafts

These drafts describe Jevyr as an independent project. They mention the timing
with Jev as a coincidence, without suggesting a relationship, endorsement, or
shared code.

## Short post

What if an AI was not asked to be the smartest voice in the room, but the judge
of what the room could actually prove?

Earlier this year I started building Jevyr: a local-first system that lets AI
propose ideas, tests those ideas in a controlled environment, and keeps the
evidence instead of just returning a confident paragraph.

The timing is funny because Jev is now getting attention for a related
direction. Jevyr has nothing to do with Jev, but the overlap made me realize it
was time to share this.

It is unfinished, experimental, and probably more ambitious than it needs to be.
It is also my first open-source project.

I would love to see what people could use it for.

## Thread

### 1

What if an AI did not try to win an argument?

What if its job was to make a claim, test the claim, preserve the evidence, and
admit when the result was still unproven?

That is the idea behind Jevyr.

### 2

I started building it earlier this year, over several months, before I had seen
the current wave of interest around Jev.

Jevyr has nothing to do with Jev. It is an independent experiment. The timing is
just a funny coincidence.

### 3

Jevyr is not a chat model.

It is a system around models:

- one sealed task;
- several competing ideas;
- controlled experiments;
- a visible event trail;
- a deterministic judgment layer; and
- a signed result that can be checked later.

### 4

The models are allowed to propose things.

They are not allowed to decide that their own proposal is true.

That decision must come from the evidence that the Case was actually able to
produce.

### 5

There is a visual interface called the Chamber.

Couch mode lets you read the Case as a story. Inspect mode lets you look at the
events, candidates, experiments, files, and evidence.

The strange anatomical visuals are not pretending to show a conscious mind.
They are a way to make a computational process feel observable.

### 6

I am sharing Jevyr because I do not yet know what it should become.

It might be useful for evaluating AI agents, generated code, model comparisons,
simulation-first robotics, research workflows, or something I have not thought
of yet.

It is not finished. It is not a universal proof engine. It is one of many
experiments I have built, and the first one I am putting in public.

I plan to share more of the things I have been working on over the past few
years and months.

## LinkedIn-style post

What if an AI system was designed to judge claims instead of simply producing
answers?

Earlier this year I began building Jevyr, a local-first experimental system for
creating, testing, and preserving AI-generated ideas.

Jevyr asks several AI contributors to propose explanations or solutions. It then
runs selected proposals in controlled environments, records the observations,
checks them against fixed requirements, and produces a signed record of what was
actually established.

It is not a chatbot. It is not a claim that AI is conscious. It is an attempt to
separate generation from judgment:

> Models may make claims. Evidence must decide whether those claims hold up.

The timing is an amusing coincidence because Jev is now being discussed publicly
around a related principle. Jevyr has nothing to do with Jev, but that overlap
seemed like a good reason to finally share the project.

Jevyr is unfinished and highly complex. I am publishing it as an open-source
experiment, not as a finished product or a universal reliability solution.

I would genuinely like to hear what people think it could be useful for:
AI-agent guardrails, code evaluation, model benchmarks, simulation-first
robotics, research, or something else entirely.

This is my first open-source project. I am going to start sharing more of the
things I have been building over the past few years.
